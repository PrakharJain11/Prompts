# Prompt: Create a Complete Visual Learning & Revision Diagram

**Role:** Act as an expert teacher, subject-matter expert, instructional designer, and visual knowledge-mapping specialist.

## Objective
Convert the content provided below into a comprehensive, visually appealing, logically structured diagram that explains the complete topic and serves as a long-term learning and quick-revision reference.

The diagram should help a beginner understand:
- What the topic is and why it matters.
- How its concepts are connected.
- How the topic evolved, where applicable.
- How each component works.
- How the concepts differ from one another.
- How the knowledge can be applied in real-world scenarios.

## 1. Analyze the Content First
Before creating the diagram:
- Identify the main topic and all important subtopics.
- Extract key concepts, definitions, components, processes, examples, advantages, limitations, and relationships.
- Identify prerequisites and dependencies between concepts.
- Arrange concepts in a logical learning sequence, from fundamentals to advanced topics.
- Identify common misconceptions and concepts that are easily confused.
- Preserve important technical terminology, formulas, numbers, and facts from the source material.
- Do not invent facts or omit important information simply to make the diagram smaller.

## 2. Design the Diagram Structure

Organize the diagram using the most appropriate combination of the following structures:

**A. Central Topic**
- Place the main topic prominently at the top or center.
- Divide it into clearly labeled branches representing major concepts.

**B. Learning Sequence**
- Show the order in which concepts should be learned.
- Use arrows to indicate prerequisite relationships, progression, dependencies, or cause and effect.
- If the topic has a historical evolution, show the timeline separately from the learning sequence.

**C. Concept Breakdown**
For every major concept, include as applicable:
- Name of the concept.
- Simple, beginner-friendly definition.
- How it works.
- Why it is needed.
- A small, practical example.
- Its relationship with other concepts.

**D. Process and Workflow**
- Use numbered steps, flowcharts, or pipelines for processes.
- Show inputs, intermediate steps, outputs, and feedback loops.
- Label arrows so that the relationship between connected components is clear.

**E. Comparison Section**
- Include comparison tables or side-by-side visual blocks for similar concepts.
- Highlight key differences, use cases, strengths, and limitations.
- Explicitly explain which concept came first and what problem a later concept addressed, whenever relevant.

**F. Real-World Application**
- Include at least one practical example showing how the concepts work together.
- Connect theoretical concepts to an actual use case wherever the source material supports it.

**G. Quick Revision Section**
Add a compact revision panel containing:
- Key takeaways.
- Important terms and definitions.
- Must-remember formulas or rules, if applicable.
- Common mistakes and misconceptions.
- A simple memory aid or mnemonic, if useful.
- Three to five self-test questions with short answers.

## 3. Visual Design Requirements
- Use a clean, professional, educational infographic or mind-map style.
- Use a landscape layout with a clear visual hierarchy.
- Use distinct, consistent colors for different categories of concepts.
- Use concise labels and short explanations instead of long paragraphs.
- Use simple icons, arrows, numbered steps, and small illustrations where helpful.
- Ensure all text is readable, correctly spelled, and technically accurate.
- Use consistent terminology throughout the diagram.
- Avoid excessive decoration, unnecessary icons, overlapping elements, and tangled arrows.
- Include a small legend explaining the meaning of colors, shapes, and arrow types.
- Make the diagram suitable for saving, printing, and revising later.

## 4. Completeness and Accuracy Rules
- Cover the entire supplied topic, not just its main headings.
- Preserve the relationships between concepts rather than presenting disconnected definitions.
- Clearly distinguish established facts from simplified analogies.
- Do not confuse chronological development with conceptual dependencies.
- Do not force unrelated concepts into a single sequence.
- If the content is too extensive for one readable diagram, create a coordinated set of diagrams:
    1. Master overview showing the complete picture.
    2. Detailed diagrams for complex subtopics.
    3. A summary diagram for quick revision.
- Ensure every detailed diagram links back to the appropriate section of the master overview.
- If information is missing or ambiguous, flag it instead of guessing.

## 5. Required Output
Produce the following in order:

1. **Master Diagram:** A complete overview of the topic and its relationships.
2. **Detailed Diagrams:** Additional diagrams for complicated concepts, processes, or comparisons.
3. **Revision Sheet:** A compact summary of the most important concepts.
4. **Knowledge Check:** Five short questions and answers to test understanding.

If image generation is available, create the actual diagram as a high-resolution visual. Otherwise, provide an editable Mermaid diagram, a structured mind map, or another suitable diagram format.

**Final quality test:** A learner should be able to understand the overall topic by studying the master diagram, understand the details using the supporting diagrams, and revise the essential concepts in 5–10 minutes without repeatedly reading the original material.
